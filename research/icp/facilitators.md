# ICP — Facilitators

**Method and limits.** Desk research on the public web, 2026-11-16. No interviews were held; nothing here is an interview finding. In this session direct page fetches were blocked by the network allowlist, so all evidence comes from web-search result extracts. Quotes are reproduced exactly as the search tool returned them and have **not** been checked against the live page; treat each as "extract, to be verified". No Reddit, LinkedIn or App Store review text could be retrieved, so first-person practitioner voice is thin. Anything not sourced is labelled **Hypothesis**.

## 1. Summary

Best-fit sub-segment: **solo, in-person facilitators of single-block, discussion-led sessions of roughly 60–120 minutes** (support, therapy and circle groups; community meetings; short client workshops), who have no co-facilitator to signal time and for whom looking at a watch or phone reads as impatience. Their existing practice of time warnings at "ten, five and two minutes" [3] maps directly onto Nudgy's nudges-before-the-end model. Workshop facilitators who run many short timeboxes on a shared, visible timer are a poorer match.

**Overall fit: Moderate.** The need for private time cues is evidenced, but the dominant norm is a timer everyone can see, most agendas are multi-timebox, and typical sessions exceed the 1-hour limit for distinct Watch tap patterns.

## 3. How they keep time today

- **Co-facilitator or timekeeper with hand signals.** Works when a second person exists. Fails for the majority who work alone [2], and a timekeeper only informs; the facilitator still decides [6].
- **Shared visible timer** (physical Time Timer, projected countdown, Miro/Mural/retro-tool timers). The mainstream answer [8][14][15]. Fails where pressure is unwanted: one UX course states that a visible countdown "can create a pressured environment" [10], an LSP practitioner's projected countdown drew the objection that it makes participants anxious [20], and Jake Knapp is quoted: "I wouldn't want to conduct a job interview with a Time Timer" [14]. A Miro wish-list post from a frequent workshop facilitator asks for a timer visible only to the facilitator, with a nudge at each pre-planned milestone [7] (paraphrase; wording not retrieved).
- **Phone timer.** Not visible to the group: "you'll have to be more proactive about giving people timing updates if they can't see the timer" [11]. **Hypothesis:** an audible phone alarm or a visible phone check is the failure Nudgy addresses; no first-person source found.
- **Wall clock or wristwatch.** Requires looking away and mental arithmetic; contrasted with the Time Timer where "no math is required" [14].
- **Session-planning tools.** SessionLab's Time Tracker moves through agenda blocks and lets the facilitator add time; paid plans only [13]. Screen-bound.
- **Apple Watch haptic timers already on the App Store** (listing data only, few or no ratings): QuietSession — iPhone plus Watch, "quiet haptic cues on your wrist as the session approaches its end", aimed at therapists, coaches and counsellors, free with a Pro in-app purchase (price not retrieved) [12]; PREMER — free, vibrates at four evenly spaced intervals, development discontinued per its repository [21]; HapticClock (free, interval taps), Shush ($0.99), Haptica, VibeCraft [22]; TheraTime (free, Watch-only, massage therapists) [23]; Silo Zen ($2.99) [24]. "Facilitator Timer" targets workshop leaders but is an iPad/Mac app with two timers, not a Watch app [22]. **None of these could be tested; whether any offers distinct tap patterns per nudge is unknown.**

## 4. Pains and jobs to be done

1. **No one to signal time when working alone.** The recommended fix assumes a partner: "ask them to hold up 10 fingers for "10 minutes left."" [3]; "to signal to your co-facilitator that they're running out of time on an exercise or discussion" [4]. Yet 97% sometimes or mostly work alone [2].
2. **Clock-watching damages presence.** Seeds for Change is reported as advising against signs of impatience "such as looking at your watch, or flicking through your notes" [5] (extract not reconfirmed on a second search).
3. **Too many things to track at once.** A facilitator-training challenge catalogue carries the entry: "As a facilitator, how do I remember to do all the things like keep time, track turns, Etc.?" [9].
4. **Falling behind late in the session.** A practitioner post is titled "It's 4:45, and You're Twenty Minutes Behind" [25]; MITRE's toolkit team writes "we must guard our time fiercely" [8].
5. **Public timers create pressure in reflective work.** "Using a visible countdown time can create a pressured environment, making it harder to brainstorm and be creative. Instead, announce the time limit for an exercise and keep your eye on the clock." [10]

## 5. Triggers

Sourced situations: working without a co-facilitator [2][3]; a session that ended behind schedule [25]; a setting where a visible timer is judged inappropriate [10][14][20]. **Hypotheses:** first solo paid engagement; participant feedback about clock-watching; moving from online (on-screen timer always in view) back to in-person; buying an Apple Watch for other reasons.

## 6. Device reality

- **Ownership.** No data on Apple Watch ownership among facilitators was found. General context only: a headline figure that 35% of iPhone users have a smartwatch [26]; Counterpoint (2023) that nearly 80% of iPhone-owning smartwatch users have an Apple Watch [27]. **Hypothesis:** ownership among freelance facilitators is at or above this.
- **Remote work.** Facilitation is "multi-modal" across in-person, online and asynchronous [1]; agile teams are majority hybrid or remote [19]. Online, a timer is already on screen; the value of a wrist tap is weaker. In-person share per sub-segment not retrieved.
- **Session length — partial disqualifier.** The typical session is ~90 minutes [1]; group therapy 60–120 [17]; retrospectives up to 3 hours [18]. Most sessions therefore exceed the 1-hour window for distinct tap patterns, and the warnings that matter (the last ten minutes) fall in the degraded part.
- **Multiple timeboxes — main model risk.** Workshop, sprint, LS and LSP facilitation consists of many short timeboxes (1–5 min rounds [16][20]; 20–30 min activities [14]); the Miro request is for a nudge at each milestone of a minute-by-minute agenda [7]. "Up to 6 nudges before the end of one session" does not model this, and agenda segments are not built. **Hypothesis:** running each activity as its own short preset is a workaround, since activity timeboxes sit under 1 hour; the restart friction is untested.
- **Group must hear transitions.** In round-based methods the signal is for participants, not only the facilitator [20]; a private tap does not replace it.

## 7. Willingness to pay

- **Who pays.** The 2023 State of Facilitation reported about half of respondents as freelancers [2]; in 2025, 31.4% facilitate in-house and 23.4% within facilitation consultancies [1]. **Hypothesis:** an app-store purchase of this size is self-paid in all cases.
- **Comparables.** SessionLab: $15 and $23 per member per month, billed annually, per search snapshots of its pricing page; plan names conflict across sources [13]. Time Timer MOD Sprint Edition: $36.95 [28]. IAF membership: $200 per year standard rate [29]. Agenda Timer (iOS): $4.49 full-access unlock [22]. Shush $0.99 [22]; Silo Zen $2.99 [24]. Several Watch haptic timers are free [21][22][23].
- **Derived range.** One-time timer-app purchases found sit between $0.99 and $4.49; facilitators also pay about $37 for a physical timer and $180+ a year for planning software. No recommendation is made. Free direct substitutes exist.

Taken from the extracts: "time warnings"; "10 minutes left"; "running out of time"; "running long"; "keep time"; "timekeeper"; "timebox"; "time check"; "keep your eye on the clock"; "looking at your watch"; "twenty minutes behind"; "guard our time"; "stay on track"; "pressured environment". ## 10. Anti-ICP

- Remote-only facilitators and retro facilitators with a tool-embedded shared timer [15][19].
- Design-sprint facilitators who want time pressure to be public [14].
- LS and LSP practitioners running many 1–5 minute rounds that the whole group must hear [16][20].
- Facilitators who always work with a co-facilitator or producer [3][4].
- Facilitators who need a re-flowing agenda (SessionLab Time Tracker users) [13].
- Android users and anyone without an iPhone (product constraint).

## 11. Riskiest assumptions

1. **That the single-session, nudges-before-end model fits facilitation at all**, given multi-timebox agendas. Test: ask 10 facilitators to map their last session onto one preset; count how many can.
2. **That facilitators want a private signal rather than a shared one.** Ask: "Who needs to know the time is nearly up — you or the room?"
3. **That sessions over 1 hour with standard notification taps are still acceptable.** Test: a 90-minute pilot on the Watch; ask whether the taps were felt and distinguishable.
4. **That target facilitators own an iPhone and Apple Watch and wear it while facilitating.** Screener question in any survey or community poll.
5. **That clock-watching is a felt pain, not just textbook advice.** Ask for the last time timekeeping went wrong and what they did next.
6. **That they will pay when free haptic timers exist.** Fake-door price test; ask what they have paid for timers.
7. **That the quotes hold.** Open every source page and confirm wording before reuse in marketing.
