# ICP — Trainers

**Method note.** Desk research on the public web, 2026-11-16. No interviews were held; nothing here is an interview finding. In this session page fetching was blocked by the network policy, so every source was read only as the excerpt returned by web search, not as the full live page. Quotes are copied from those excerpts and should be re-checked against the page before being used in marketing. Reddit, LinkedIn and App Store review text could not be reached; first-person practitioner evidence is therefore thin. Numbers in square brackets refer to section 12.

## 1. Summary

Best-fit sub-segment: **independent trainers and speakers who deliver in-person, timed slots of up to about 60 minutes** — conference and association talks, guest sessions, and single modules inside a longer course — who already own an iPhone and Apple Watch and pay for their own tools. Their problem is documented (rooms without a visible clock, slide software hiding the time, checking a phone looking bad) and several small apps already sell wrist haptics to exactly this group. Full-day course trainers have the same pain but collide with Nudgy's one-hour limit for distinct tap patterns.

**Overall fit: Moderate.** The pain and the haptic solution are both evidenced for presenters, but no first-person evidence was found of trainers asking for wrist nudges, typical training blocks exceed one hour, and Apple Watch owners are a minority of iPhone owners.

## 3. How they keep time today

- **Wall clock / room monitor.** Often absent or unreadable: one speaker notes the room monitor may not exist at a venue and may not be precise [2]; another that the room may have no clock or one not easily visible [1].
- **Wristwatch.** Recommended as standard kit [18][19], but a glance is visible. One guide suggests wearing the face on the inside of the wrist to hide the check [1].
- **Phone.** Described as cumbersome and noticeable on stage [1]. Toastmasters' magazine calls the smartphone the most common timing device in its clubs, but there it is operated by a separate timer, not the speaker [13].
- **Laptop / presenter view.** Fails once slides run full screen [2]; the workaround in that post is a second device or on-screen break timer placed in view, sometimes on the floor [2].
- **A person.** A dedicated timekeeper or co-facilitator is advised because a lead trainer doing both lets the schedule slip [15]; virtual sessions give this to a producer [17]. Fails for solo trainers.
- **Visible countdowns.** Stage timers and confidence monitors (Stagetimer: about $10 per event or $12–15 per month, third-party figures) [21]. One facilitator prefers a private watch over a public timer so timings can flex [19].
- **Haptic hardware.** Logitech Spotlight remote vibrates five minutes before the end and at the end; about $97–120 [9]. Reviews are favourable about the vibration [10]. A study found a vibrating remote gave the best overall time adherence against a visual timer [12].
- **Apple Watch haptic apps (direct competitors).** TalkPace, per-slide wrist cues, $4.99 [6]; Shush, $0.99 [7]; Speaker Presentation Timer, free with $19.99 Pro purchases, 2 US ratings averaging 1.0 with a complaint that the upgrade failed [8]; Haptix, free, sectioned routines [11]; HapticClock, taps every 15 minutes, free [7]; Haptica, free with in-app purchases [7]. These listings show few or no ratings — evidence of supply, not of demand.
- **Built-in Apple Watch.** Taptic Time taps out the current time on request; it is not a countdown [20].

## 4. Pains and jobs to be done

1. **No reliable clock in the room.** "The room might not have a clock, or it might not be easily visible." [1]
2. **Slides hide the time.** "while Powerpoint's running, you'll have a tough time checking the time" [2]
3. **Checking a phone reads badly.** "It is cumbersome, noticeable and might give the impression that you are checking your messages." [1]
4. **Overrun damages credibility.** "Never go over time, ever." [4] "I never saw a congregation that was excited about me running 10, 15, or 20 minutes over in delivering my sermon." [3]
5. **Chronic running out of time.** A trainer blog post is titled with the complaint it hears from trainers: "I always run out of time" [5]. Its remedy is planning and rehearsal, not a device — the pain may be seen as a design problem, not a clock problem.
6. **Solo delivery.** Advice assumes a timekeeper or producer [15][17]; no quote found from solo trainers. **Hypothesis.**

## 5. Triggers

Evidence-linked, but each is a **Hypothesis** as a purchase trigger:
- Overrunning, or being cut off, in a recent session [3][4].
- A booking in an unfamiliar venue with no confidence monitor [1][2].
- Moving from co-delivered to solo delivery [15].
- Buying an Apple Watch and searching the App Store for a presentation timer [6][7].
- A tightly timed format (lightning talk, pitch, assessed speech) [13].

## 6. Device reality

- **Ownership.** No data found on trainers specifically. General figures: about 18% of iPhone owners owned an Apple Watch (CIRP, 2023); a higher figure of 30% for 2022 was also reported but could not be confirmed [23]. Treat ownership as a minority filter on every sub-segment.
- **Wearability.** In-person classroom and stage settings place no obvious restriction on a watch; watches are already recommended kit [18][19]. Restricted sites (secure or clean environments) were not researched.
- **Session length — the main constraint.** Full days run 8–9 hours, half days about 3 hours, and single blocks are advised at up to 90 minutes [15][16]. Nudgy's distinct patterns hold for one hour with the app open. Apple's developer forum material is consistent with this being a platform limit: background-capable extended runtime sessions carry a one-hour limit [24]. A trainer would need to start a new session per module. Whether that is acceptable is unknown.
- **Remote delivery.** The time is on screen and a producer may keep it [17]; the Watch adds little.
- **Disqualifiers.** Android users; trainers without a Watch (iPhone taps in a pocket are untested); those who need one unbroken timer across a 90-minute or longer block with distinguishable patterns.

## 7. Willingness to pay

- **Who pays.** Freelancers pay themselves; UK contract trainer day rates in job adverts have a median of about £380–400 (IT-sector postings, a loose proxy) [25]. Employed trainers: unknown whether a personal app is expensed. **Hypothesis:** a one-time low price avoids the question.
- **Comparables.** Watch haptic timer apps: free to $0.99 to $4.99, with one at $19.99 [6][7][8][11]. Interval timer one-time purchase: $7.99 [22]. Haptic presenter hardware: about $97–120 [9]. Event timer SaaS: about $10 per event [21].
- **Derived range.** Direct app comparables span $0.99–$19.99 one-time, clustering at or below $7.99. Several competitors are free, which caps what an undifferentiated timer can charge. No price is recommended here.

## 9. Their language

From the sources: "run out of time"; "running over"; "go over time"; "stay within the allotted time slot"; "keep to time"; "time slot"; "hard stop"; "timekeeper"; "checking your messages" (the impression to avoid); "noticeable"; "discreet"; "buffer". Competitor wording: "silent", "stealthily vibrate", "without breaking eye contact", "timing you can feel" [6][7][8][11].

## 10. Anti-ICP

- Fitness and personal trainers (interval repeats, audible cues, mature paid category) [22].
- Webinar hosts and virtual trainers with an on-screen clock or a producer [17].
- Trainers working with a co-facilitator or timekeeper [15].
- Event-run stages with a confidence monitor or stage timer [21].
- Trainers whose blocks run 90 minutes or more and who will not restart a timer per module [15][16].
- Android users and trainers with no Apple Watch.
- Trainers who want per-agenda-item or per-slide timing (not built; TalkPace offers per-slide cues) [6].

## 11. Riskiest assumptions

1. **Trainers want a wrist nudge at all, rather than better session design.** Only vendor listings and advice articles were found, no practitioner asking for it. Interview: "Tell me about the last session that ran over. What did you do about it afterwards?"
2. **The one-hour limit is tolerable.** Test: ask five full-day trainers to run one course day with a preset per module and report whether they kept restarting it.
3. **Enough of the segment owns an Apple Watch.** Test: a one-question poll in a trainer community on phone and watch owned.
4. **A paid app can compete with free haptic timers.** Test: landing page with two price points; compare click-through. Also install Haptix, HapticClock and TalkPace and record what they fail at.
5. **Taps are noticed while speaking and moving.** The supporting study concerned a hand-held remote, not a wrist [12]. Test: ten rehearsed talks, count missed nudges.
6. **Speakers, not trainers, are the real entry segment.** Test: run the same message in a speaking community and a trainer community; compare sign-ups.
7. **Employed trainers can install and use a personal app at work.** Interview: "Who decides what is on the phone or watch you wear when training?"
