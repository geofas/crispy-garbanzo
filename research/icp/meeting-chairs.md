# ICP — Meeting chairs

**Method and limits.** Desk research on the public web, 2026-11-16. No interviews were held; nothing here is an interview finding. In this session only web search worked: opening pages directly was blocked by the network policy, and Reddit was not searchable. Every sourced claim therefore rests on search-result excerpts of the cited URL, not on a full read of the page. Quotes are given as they appeared in those excerpts and should be re-checked against the page before use in marketing. Anything not seen in a source is labelled **Hypothesis**. Bracketed numbers refer to section 12.

## 1. Summary

Best-fit sub-segment: **conference session chairs and panel moderators running in-person sessions** — one person, visible to an audience, responsible for several speakers each with a fixed slot, and expected to give warnings at set points before each slot ends. Published chairing guidelines prescribe exactly the schedule Nudgy delivers (e.g. warnings at 5, 3, 1 and 0 minutes) [5][6][8], slots are well inside the one-hour limit for distinct tap patterns, and looking at a phone on stage is awkward. The weakness is frequency: most chair a few sessions a year (**Hypothesis**, not sourced). Second-best: in-person board and committee chairs without a timekeeper.

**Overall fit: Moderate.** The duty to keep time is well documented, but most chairs already cover it with free means (a timekeeper colleague, phone stopwatch, cards, on-screen clocks), and no source showed chairs asking for a private wrist cue.

## 2. Sub-segments

| Sub-segment | Typical session | Why timing matters | Fit | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conference session chairs | Several talks with fixed slots per session; in-person; occasional | Overruns take time from later speakers and Q&A; chair must signal at fixed points | **High** | Guidelines prescribe 3/1/0-minute or 5/1-minute cards [5][6]; overrun called "disrespectful to the other presenters and to the audience" [7] |
| Panel moderators | One panel plus Q&A; in-person, on stage | Must hit halfway and last-five-minutes marks while conducting the conversation | **High–Med** | Guides advise deciding checkpoints in advance and warning panellists [9][10] |
| Board / committee / charity / council chairs | Timed agenda; often in-person; length and frequency not sourced (**Hypothesis**: monthly to quarterly, often over one hour) | Chair must keep items to time while also steering the discussion | **Med** | Guides recommend a separate timekeeper giving a 2-minute warning per item so the chair need not both facilitate and watch the clock [11][12] |
| Team leads / managers, recurring meetings | Not sourced (**Hypothesis**: 30–60 min, weekly, largely remote or hybrid) | Back-to-back calendars | **Med–Low** | A survey of 948 executives and managers found meetings often do not start and finish on time (secondary source, percentages unverified) [13] |
| Scrum Masters / agile coaches | Daily Scrum, 15 min; other timeboxed events | Timebox is part of the framework | **Low** | Guidance favours a timer visible to the whole team and shared enforcement [14][15][16]; Scrum.org says the Developers conduct the Daily Scrum [17] |

## 3. How they keep time today

- **A timekeeper colleague.** Board guides recommend a timekeeper who is not the chair or secretary [11][12]. Fails when nobody is available or willing; it is a workaround for the chair being unable to do both jobs.
- **Phone stopwatch or timer.** "Have a way to keep track of time, whether a separate timer or a stopwatch on your phone." [18]. One guide notes some chairs use phone timers with vibration alerts so they can cue the time card without constantly checking the clock [19]. Fails because the phone must be looked at or lie on the table, and it may be needed for other things.
- **Time cards and signals.** Colour-coded 3-minute, 1-minute and end cards [5]; 5- and 1-minute cards [6]; signals agreed beforehand [8]. These signal the speaker; the chair still needs a prompt of their own to raise the card on time.
- **Visible shared timers.** Agile guides advise starting a visible timer [14]; browser stand-up timers exist [20]; stage-timer services serve conferences [21]. Fails in rooms without a screen and offers no discretion.
- **Apple Watch built-ins.** Taptic Time taps out the current time on request [22][23]; silent taps on the hour or half hour are possible [24]. Neither is relative to a session end. An Apple Community user asking for a vibration 10 minutes before appointments was pointed to per-event calendar alerts, set one by one [25].
- **Competing Apple Watch haptic timers** (App Store listings, vendor descriptions):
  - HapticClock — free, Watch-only; enter the meeting end time; "gentle, discreet taps on your wrist at 15-minute intervals"; too few ratings to display [1].
  - Haptica — free with in-app purchases; lifetime $9.99, annual $4.99; 3 ratings [2].
  - Tap Timer — lifetime $19.99, annual $17.99, monthly $2.99, weekly $0.99 [3].
  - Shush: Silent Haptic Timer — $0.99 [4].
  - TalkPace, a haptic presentation timer for Apple Watch and iPhone [26].
  - Where they fail: a reviewer of one haptic timer complained it "would not work when my watch face turned off" [4]; a developer reported of a watchOS 10 runtime session, "I get about 50 seconds when the screen goes dark, and then the timer just stops." [27]. HapticClock's fixed 15-minute interval cannot give a 5-minute warning [1].

The category is occupied but small: the listings seen carry very few ratings [1][2].

## 4. Pains and jobs to be done

Verbatim first-person statements from chairs were scarce in what could be reached; most evidence is guidance written for chairs. Treat the pains as documented duties, not confirmed felt pain.

1. **Chairing and clock-watching compete.** Guides separate the roles so the chair does not have to both facilitate and watch the clock [11]. No first-person quote found.
2. **Checking the time looks rude.** A blogger using Taptic Time in meetings wrote (paraphrased in the excerpt, not verbatim) that looking at the time in a meeting is rude because it suggests you cannot wait to get away [23]. This is a meeting participant, not specifically a chair.
3. **Overruns are the chair's fault and are unfair to others.** Allowing more than the allotted time is "disrespectful to the other presenters and to the audience" [7].
4. **Warnings must come at precise points.** "color-coded cards to indicate 3-minute, 1-minute, and end (0-minutes) countdown times" [5].
5. **Existing watch timers are not trusted.** "would not work when my watch face turned off" [4].

## 5. Triggers

Sourced only as situations described in guidance; that they prompt a search is a **Hypothesis**.
- Being appointed session chair or moderator and receiving the organiser's chairing guidelines [5][6][8].
- A session that overran and cut Q&A or a later speaker [7].
- Taking on a chair role with a timed agenda and no timekeeper [11][12].
- Already owning an Apple Watch and discovering haptic time features [22][23][24].

## 6. Device reality

- **Ownership.** No source gives Apple Watch ownership by job role. In a CIRP survey of recent US iPhone buyers, about 37% also owned an Apple Watch [28]. Counterpoint's 2023 US study found nearly 80% of smartwatch users with an iPhone own an Apple Watch — a share of smartwatch owners, not of all iPhone owners [29]. Smartwatch or tracker wear rises with income (31% above $75k versus 12% below $30k; Pew via a secondary source, year unclear) [30]. Reading: a substantial minority of iPhone-owning chairs, not a majority. Android users are excluded outright.
- **Setting.** Board etiquette guidance allows devices for meeting purposes and asks that phones be silenced [31][32]; no governance source found bans smartwatches. One commercial style guide advises against wearing a smartwatch alone at boardroom-level client meetings and recommends turning haptic notifications off [33] — weak evidence of a dress-norm risk.
- **Remote and hybrid.** Owl Labs 2025: 63% fully in-office, 28% hybrid, 9% fully remote [34]; in a Prezi survey 47.3% said most of their meetings are hybrid and 45% that most are fully remote [35] (vendor surveys, conflicting). On a video call a clock is already on screen, so the discreet-cue benefit is small (**Hypothesis**).
- **Disqualifiers.** Meetings over one hour lose distinct tap patterns and get standard notification taps — a direct problem for long board meetings and for conference sessions timed as one block. Reliability with the display off is an observed failure in this category [4][27].

## 7. Willingness to pay

- **Who pays.** Not established. **Hypothesis**: self-paid, since this is a personal-device utility; volunteer and academic chairs have no budget line for it.
- **Comparables (US App Store unless stated).** Shush $0.99 one-off [4]; The Timekeeper $2.99 [36]; Fin speech timer $9.99 per year [36]; Speech Timer for Toastmasters custom speech types $9.99 in-app [36]; Haptica $9.99 lifetime or $4.99 per year [2]; Tap Timer $19.99 lifetime [3]; HapticClock free [1].
- **Employer-paid tools.** Parabol $8 per active user per month [37]; Stagetimer Pro $17.50 per month billed annually [38].
- **Range from comparables.** One-time prices for personal haptic or speech timers run from $0.99 to $19.99, with two direct comparables at $9.99. A free direct competitor exists [1]. No price recommendation is made.

## 10. Anti-ICP

- Scrum Masters whose teams use a shared visible timer; the point is that everyone sees it [14][16].
- Chairs of mostly remote meetings (**Hypothesis**: on-screen clock suffices).
- Chairs with a dedicated timekeeper or AV-run stage timer [11][21].
- Android users and iPhone users without an Apple Watch who want wrist cues.
- Chairs of meetings regularly over one hour who need distinct patterns throughout.
- Chairs who need per-agenda-item timing or calendar import (not built).

## 11. Riskiest assumptions

1. **Chairs want a private cue rather than a shared, visible one.** No first-person demand found. Test: five interviews with recent session chairs — "Last time, how did you know when to give the five-minute warning? What went wrong?"
2. **Session chairs chair often enough to install and pay.** Test: ask frequency per year; landing page aimed at session chairs with a waiting list.
3. **Enough chairs own an Apple Watch and wear it in these settings.** Test: screener question in outreach to conference chairs and board chairs.
4. **The one-hour pattern limit does not exclude the main use.** Test: collect real session and slot lengths from ten conference programmes and ten board agendas.
5. **A free competitor (HapticClock) and $0.99–$9.99 comparables leave room for a paid unlock.** Test: price-point test on the landing page.
6. **Nudges are reliable with the display off during a real session.** Test: device trial across ten sessions by three chairs.
7. **Reddit and forum sentiment** was unreachable. Test: manual read of scrum and governance communities.
